# Automation as maturity

Capabilities are ranked by how automated they are. Human-realized
capabilities are recorded as immature, with automation as the
destination. Progress is reported as the percentage automated.

## Why it is tempting

It is measurable, it produces a clean narrative, and it is often
partially true. Automating a repetitive provisioning path really does
help.

The ladder also matches how funding is usually argued. "We automated 60
percent of requests" is a sentence that survives a budget review.
"We decided this judgment should stay human" is not.

## What it costs

| Cost | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Encoding unsettled judgment | Automating a decision that still varies produces confident wrong answers |
| Devaluing real capabilities | A mature human-realized assessment capability is recorded as a deficiency |
| Perverse incentive | Teams automate what is easy to automate rather than what matters |
| Hidden fragility | An automated path with no living expertise behind it looks strong until its assumptions break |

## How to recognize it

- A roadmap where every human-realized capability has "automate" as its
  next step.
- Automation percentage reported as a maturity measure.
- Specialists describing their own work as technical debt.
- No capability has ever been deliberately left human.

## What to do instead

**Capability maturity is not automation maturity.** Consider
explicitness, repeatability, degree of human judgment, organizational
independence, composability, governance, and observability
[independently](../02-capabilities/capability-characteristics.md), as
spectra rather than a score.

`AssessNovelSecurityArchitecture` should stay human-realized. Encoding
its recurring conclusions is progress. Automating the judgment itself is
not, and would be
[encoding an unsettled decision](../12-method/deciding-what-to-encode.md).
