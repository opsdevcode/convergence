# Catalog first

The first deliverable is a complete capability catalog. Months go into
enumerating, categorizing, and modelling every capability in the
organization before any single path is changed.

## Why it is tempting

Completeness feels like rigor, and a catalog is a legible artifact that
can be shown to leadership. It also feels like the responsible order of
operations: understand the whole before changing a part.

Tooling encourages it. Most catalog products invite population as the
first activity.

## What it costs

| Cost | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| An inventory of aspirations | Entries describe intended abilities, unvalidated by any real consumer |
| Stale on arrival | The organization changed during the modelling exercise |
| No feedback | Nothing was traced, so no assumption was tested against a real flow |
| Exhausted patience | Sponsorship is spent before anything improves for anyone |

Completeness also invites false confidence. A catalog that lists
everything implies the model is understood, when the entries were mostly
guessed.

**Useful incompleteness is better than fictional completeness.**

## How to recognize it

- The catalog has more entries than the organization has traced flows.
- Entries have owners who were assigned rather than consulted.
- Debate concerns taxonomy and naming conventions rather than any
  specific consumer's experience.
- No consumer has yet used anything differently.

## What to do instead

Trace one flow. Name the two or three capabilities it reveals. Encode
what is settled. Then repeat. The catalog, if one is ever needed, is a
byproduct of having done this several times.

A [capability graph](../02-capabilities/capability-graph.md) may be
partial, federated, or scoped. No single enterprise graph, registry, or
global taxonomy is required. See
[Brownfield adoption](../11-adoption/brownfield.md) and the
[method](../12-method/README.md).
